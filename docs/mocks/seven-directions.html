Seven directions for the CCC landing page

Same slice of the page in every one — eyebrow, headline, video, objection chips, the two situations, one case card. Only the design system changes. Palette, type pairing, spacing rhythm, button shape, card treatment and label style are each a complete, internally consistent set.

Copy is the real thing from the strategy brief. The case card is a placeholder, marked as such — no verified client goes on a mock. Pick one to develop, or name the parts you want spliced from two.

Direction 01

Night Desk

The James Smith lineage, stripped of the countdown clock. Near-black ground, one saturated blue doing every job a colour can do, generous 16px radii, and a single grotesk at two weights. Says: this is a professional operation that works at night while you sleep.

Strongest fit if the VSL is the centrepiece — dark grounds make video look intentional rather than pasted on. Risk: dark high-ticket pages are the house style of the coaching funnel, so it inherits that association whether or not you want it.

Direction 02

Warm Signal

Drawn from the Imperium funnel: white ground, warm grey body text instead of black, one orange for every call to action, and pastel tint blocks giving the page rhythm without introducing new colours. Centred, narrow, pill buttons. Says: approachable, plain-spoken, nothing hidden.

The friendliest of the seven, and the easiest to read for a stressed visitor. Risk: warmth can undersell a 6,000 PLN commitment if the type isn't held tight.

Direction 03

Gilt

The Goh Consulting lineage taken further: warm near-black, a single gold, a large serif set at light weight, and a narrow 720px column that never widens. Letterspaced small-caps eyebrow, square-ish buttons, almost no radius. Says: this is expensive, and it is for a particular kind of person.

The strongest signal of selectivity, which serves your qualification problem directly. Risk: it is the least warm of the seven, and an anxious visitor may read restraint as coldness.

Direction 04

Chancery

None of your five references; this one comes from the professional-services shelf — law, accountancy, executive search. White ground, navy ink, a text serif for headlines, hairline rules, 3px corners, a rail on the case card. Says: we are an institution, and our claims are precise.

The direction that best matches your claim rules — precise outcome labels look native here rather than like hedging. Risk: it reads conservative, and conservative can read as slow.

Direction 05

Grid Office

Swiss poster logic. Off-white paper, black rules, one vermilion, zero radius anywhere, and a heavy grotesk set very tight so the headline reads as a built object. Uppercase micro-labels. Says: someone with taste made this, and nothing here is accidental.

The least likely of the seven to be mistaken for a template or for AI output — which was the original brief. Risk: it is the most demanding to extend; every new section has to earn its place on the grid, and sloppy additions show immediately.

Direction 06

Green Room

A calm, human register: soft grey-green paper, deep forest ink, a clay accent, and a soft serif with real optical warmth. Rounded but not bubbly, generous line height, a rail on the case card. Says: you are talking to people, and this will not be humiliating.

Built for the emotional reality in the research — frustration at not being recognised. This is the only direction that leads with reassurance rather than authority. Risk: it is the furthest from a premium price signal, and needs the pricing section to work hard.

Direction 07

Blueprint

The only direction whose visual idea is the product itself: a system with people operating it. Cool paper ground, monospace for every label and eyebrow, faint ruled card edges, small 4px corners, one working blue. Says: there is a documented process here, and you can see it.

Answers the objection the research pack flags hardest — visitors not understanding what a done-for-you team actually does. Labels in mono make the process sections legible at a glance. Risk: mono can read as a developer tool rather than a career service.
